Richard Lonsdale
Thinking Machines: Brains, AI and our future

Luigi Di Castri

Photo by David Katan

In April 2020, Richard Lonsdale, who has a PhD in Plasma Physics and worked for 5 years as a researcher in computational neuroscience, published Knowing Your Mind: Lessons from neuroscience and artificial intelligence, a book in which he discussed the brain, the mind, and whether or not Artificial Intelligence (AI) is a brain-like computer. In the 6 years since that book was published, things have changed: AI is far more powerful, becomming even more like the brain and the human mind, perhaps surpassing them both. The ideas that Richard presented in that book, while far from obselete, are in need of an update. On Friday November 13th, 2026, Richard came to The Berkeley to do exactly that.

Richard divided his talk into two sections. In the first, he explained ‘the mechanics of thinking’ both in the brain and AI. In the second, he talked about our future: namely the potential of AI and what it might mean for humanity.

He began by explaining the brain’s purpose, essentially processing sensory information and controlling actions. He told us that the sea squirt, once it finds a suitable place to spend the rest of its life, digests much of its own brain. (He joked that this was like university professors once they gain tenure.) But how does the brain work? Richard described billions of neurons firing electrical shots at each other and coding memory and more into the synapses of the brain. The scale is astounding: the brain has 86 billion neurons and 300 trillion synapses. This is enough ‘wiring’ to wrap around the earth 20 times. One wonders how we could ever imitate such a ‘machine’.

Coming now to AI, for clarity Richard defined AI as ‘computer technology enabling machines to think, learn and do tasks that normally need human intelligence.’ He introduced Moore’s Law – the prediction that the number of transistors on a computer chip doubles every two years – which leads both to a rapid increase in computer power and to falling costs. Richard offered that this brings computer technology – and specifically AI and the internet – even closer to the brain’s potential.

Richard then went into detail about the software mechanisms of AI, how neural networks resemble the brain, and how vast amounts of training data are used to create and modify parameters in the neural networks, enabling AI to ‘predict’ textual sequences and recognise images – Richard used numbers as an example – at least as well as humans. He explained that we don’t know how the brain does this, but we think neural networks are an approximation, and that parameters and synapses are essentially performing similar functions, ‘remembering’ what things look like so they can be recognised again.

Next Richard talked about the Turing Test – the Imitation Game – created by Alan Turing in 1950. Turing’s idea was to establish whether or not a human knew they were talking to another human or to a computer. It was achieved with little fanfare by ChatGPT in 2024. This led Richard to elaborate on Large Language Models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and others. They were trained on data sourced from the internet – in the region of 10 trillion words – which took 2 months to process.

He then made some comparisons between the human brain and LLMs. In terms of capacity, unexpectedly, the brain wins, having in the region of 150 trillion synapses against 3 trillion parameters in an LLM. As for speed, we humans come a poor second; it would take a human 85,000 years to read 10 trillion words while AI takes only 2 months. Finally, humans are more efficient learners than AI, requiring much less training data. A human learns language from approximately 200 million words, compared to the 10 trillion needed to train a Large Language Model. However, through Recursive Self Improvement (RSI), effectively building itself, AI might be on the way to exceeding us at almost any intellectual endeavour.

The second part of Richard’s talk was understandably less technical and therefore shorter. Discussing AI’s potential and what it might mean for the future of humanity, he predicted that, while AI thrives in mental activities, in time there will be almost no physical task that a robot of some kind will not be able to do. Training data is problematical, manual tasks can be complex, but Richard is confident that, from plumber to electrician to, well, anything, AI will one day be able to do them all.

He then looked at some predictions that had been made about the impact of AI. Elon Musk said that there would be so much money that, by 2036, money would no longer matter. (Who could have forecast that most of it would be in his bank account?) Geoffrey Hinton, the renowned Godfather of AI, predicted massive unemployment. Ray Dalio, the financial market strategist, still argues that the AI financial bubble will burst and we’ll suffer a crash like 1929 and 2008.

Last but not least, many people in the AI community – call them 'whistleblowers', such as Jacob Coxon – have predicted that AI will end humanity, and that it could be soon. Richard addressed each of these. Previous transformational technologies took decades to fully deliver their economic benefits, and AI is likely to be similar. Fears of mass unemployment are probably overblown. For example, radiologists, whom Hinton had said we should stop training, were still thriving despite AI taking over a key part of their work. Investment in AI is very high, but not insane given the potential benefits. There may well be an AI bubble, resulting in a market crash in the near future, but the technology itself will continue to thrive.

As for destroying humanity, Richard opined that there are two threats. Firstly, AI falls into the hands of bad actors who then sabotage cyberspace or develop an evil pathogen. Secondly, the ‘alignment problem’ whereby AI, given a task, chooses a solution that unintentionally destroys us all. [The seminal reference is philosopher Nick Bostrom’s Paperclip Maximiser thought experiment.]

Richard closed this section by stating that changes arising from AI would be more significant than anyone could predict but probably slower than techno-optimists think, and that the risks to humanity are real and significant. Long-term alignment with our goals is vital.

As for the world in 2090, when Richard’s granddaughter will be his age now? He admitted to having no idea what that would be like, except that it will be very different from our world today.
